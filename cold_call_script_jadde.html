Cold call script system

Local service businesses — no website

Openings — pick one, say it like a thought
A — Default

"Hey, is this [business]? My name's Jadde — I co-own a local web design company. I came across your business doing some research — you've got strong reviews but no website. I had a thought I wanted to run by you — got about a minute?"

B — Lean into curiosity

"Hey, this might be a little random — I'm Jadde, I co-own a local web design company. I was looking at [trade] companies in the area and yours stood out. Reviews are genuinely good, but there's no website. Is that intentional, or just something that hasn't happened yet?"

C — If they sound busy or guarded

"I'll keep it short — I'm Jadde, I co-own a local web design company. Yours caught my eye because your Google reputation is solid but there's no site behind it. I had an idea — is now a decent time?"

D — If they ask "what's this about"

"Yeah, fair — I'm Jadde, I co-own a local web design company. Not trying to sell you anything right now. I noticed your reviews are strong and there's no website, and I had an idea I wanted to share. No pitch, I promise — just a thought."

Lead with what you noticed, not what you sell. You're calling because something specific caught your attention.

The core observation
Full version

"You've got solid reviews — people are clearly finding you and happy with the work. The thing is, when someone searches for [trade] and lands on a Google listing, there's nothing pushing them to actually call you specifically. A website gives them everything they need to make that decision right there: what you do, where you work, how to reach you, what past customers said. It's built to turn someone who just found you into someone who calls you."

Short version

"A Google listing gets you found. A website is what actually gets you hired."

By trade
Plumbing

"Plumbing calls are usually urgent — something's broken and people want it fixed today. When they search and find your listing, they've got a phone number. But a website that explains what you handle, your service area, and has a way to request service right there — that's a much easier yes than just a number in a Google listing."

Auto electric

"Auto electrical is a specialty — when someone has a problem they can't diagnose, they're specifically searching for someone who knows what they're doing. A website that explains what you handle and why you're the right call builds that confidence before they even pick up the phone. A listing tells them you exist. A website tells them why to choose you."

Handyman

"Handyman work is all about trust — people are letting someone into their home. When they're comparing options, the one with a real website that shows their work and explains their process is going to feel more credible than a name in a listing. A website does a lot of the trust-building before you even talk to them."

HVAC

"When someone's heat goes out or their AC dies, they're searching fast and calling whoever looks most reliable. A Google listing gives them your number — but a website gives them your services, your experience, a way to request service right then. It's the difference between 'here's a number' and 'here's why you should call it.'"

The transition

"Here's why I'm calling — and this is a little different from how this usually goes. I'm not going to ask you to commit to anything. I'd rather just build it first, and then let you decide."

The offer
Standard

"I'll build you a fully custom site — designed for your business, your services, your reviews — in three business days. Nothing out of your pocket. Once it's done, I'll send you a link. You look at it, if it feels right we talk. If not, no hard feelings. You've got nothing to lose by just seeing it."

Short version

"I build it, you look at it, you decide. Three days, nothing owed."

They're not saying yes to buying a website. They're saying yes to looking at something that already exists. Completely different ask.

If price comes up

"$1,200 for a one-pager, $2,250 for multi-page. Hosting and maintenance is $129 or $149 a month. But honestly — look at it first. If it doesn't feel worth it, the conversation stops there."

Objections — tap to expand
Validate it — genuinely.

"That's great to hear — and I'm not trying to fix something that isn't broken. The only thing I'd say is that the businesses I've seen get hit hardest are the ones that were busy right up until a slow stretch hit. A website isn't just about getting more calls — it's also there working for you when things get quieter. But if now's not the time, I get it."

Then pause. Let them fill the silence.
Don't knock it.

"Facebook's great for staying in front of people who already know you. The difference is what happens when a stranger finds you cold — they land on your Facebook page and there's no clear way to quickly figure out what you do, where you work, and how to book you. A website is built specifically for that moment. It's designed to answer those questions fast and make it easy to take the next step. Facebook's built for engagement, a website's built for conversion."

Most common thing I hear.

"Totally makes sense — it always feels like it can wait. What I'm offering takes that off the table. I do the work first. You don't manage anything or deal with back-and-forth — you just open a link and tell me what you think. I'm trying to make the starting part free."

Good sign — answer it straight.

"One-page site is $1,200, multi-page is $2,250. Hosting and maintenance after that is $129 a month. But I lead with 'see it first' because I'd rather you make that call based on what you're actually getting, not just a number."

Respect it immediately.

"Totally fair. One thing I'll say before I let you go — there's nothing to commit to upfront. But I understand if it's not the right time. Would it be alright if I checked back in a month or so?"

If they say no, just say "understood, good luck with the season" and move on.
Don't get defensive. Agree with them.

"I get that — I'd probably think the same thing. The hardest part of my job is getting someone to trust a stranger enough to take a chance. So I started building it first, at my cost, because it removes that whole barrier. There's nothing in this for me unless you're genuinely happy with it."

Closing — permission, not pressure
Best close

"Give me the go-ahead and I'll have something for you to look at by [day]. You don't have to commit to anything — just willing to take 5 minutes to look at a link. Is that something you'd be open to?"

If interested but hesitant

"What would need to be true for this to feel worth a look? I'd rather just answer that directly."

If they want time to think

"That makes sense. Can I call you back [day]? I'll keep it short."

If they say yes

"Perfect. I just need your business name, a quick description of what you do, and any photos you've got. If you don't have photos, I can work with what's public. I'll send you a link by [day]."

Never say

"So are we doing this?" / "Do we have a deal?" — kills the whole tone.

Tone and presence
Be this

Calm. Curious. Like you noticed something and wanted to share it.

Not this

Urgent. Pitchy. "Limited-time opportunity." Never.

Your role

A perceptive local professional who noticed a gap. Not a salesperson with a quota.

Their experience

They should feel seen, not sold. Like you actually looked at their business.

Silence

After a question — slow down. Let them fill it. The pause is doing work.

Firm no

"I appreciate you picking up" — and move on. No re-pitch.

The core argument

A Google listing gets you found. A website is what actually gets you hired. It's built for the moment someone's ready to make a decision — not for scrolling, not for engagement. That's the practical difference.
