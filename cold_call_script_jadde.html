Cold call script system

Local service businesses — no website

Openings — pick one, say it like a thought
A — Default

"Hey, is this [business]? My name's Jadde — I co-own a local web design company. I came across your business doing some research — you've got strong reviews but no website. I had a thought I wanted to run by you — got about a minute?"

B — Lean into curiosity

"Hey, this might be a little random — I'm Jadde, I co-own a local web design company. I was looking at [trade] companies in the area and yours stood out. Reviews are genuinely good, but there's no website. Is that intentional, or just something that hasn't happened yet?"

C — If they sound busy or guarded

"I'll keep it short — I'm Jadde, I co-own a local web design company. Yours caught my eye because your reputation is solid but there's no website behind it. I had an idea — is now a decent time?"

D — If they ask "what's this about"

"Yeah, fair — I'm Jadde, I co-own a local web design company. Not trying to sell you anything right now. I noticed your reviews are strong and there's no website, and I had a thought I wanted to share. No pitch, I promise."

Lead with what you noticed, not what you sell. You're calling because something specific caught your attention.

The core idea — internalize this, don't recite it

Most of these guys are already busy. Don't pretend they're struggling. The honest pitch isn't "you're missing business" — it's "a website works in the background without you doing anything differently, and it tends to bring in better jobs from better customers."

The core pitch
Full version

"Most guys in the trades I talk to are already busy — word of mouth does the job. I'm not calling because something's broken. What I've noticed is that the ones with a solid website tend to get a different kind of call. People who found them online come in a little more pre-sold — they've already seen the work, read the reviews, decided they want to hire you before they even pick up the phone. Less back-and-forth, less price shopping, better jobs. It's not about getting more calls — it's about the quality of the ones you get."

Short version

"Word of mouth sends whoever it sends. A website tends to attract people who are already sold before they call."

The passive angle — if they're clearly slammed

"I get it — you don't need more work right now. The thing about a website is it just runs in the background. It's there at 11pm when someone's searching, captures the people who would have hired you but couldn't find you, and when you do have a slot open, those leads are already warm. You don't have to do anything differently."

The transition

"Here's why I'm calling — and this is a little different from how this usually goes. I'm not going to ask you to commit to anything. I'd rather just build it first, and then let you decide."

The offer
Standard

"I'll build you a fully custom site — designed around your business, your services, your reviews — in three business days. Nothing out of your pocket. Once it's done, I'll send you a link. You look at it, if it feels right we talk. If not, no hard feelings. You've got nothing to lose by just seeing it."

Short version

"I build it, you look at it, you decide. Three days, nothing owed."

They're not saying yes to buying a website. They're saying yes to looking at something that already exists. Completely different ask.

If price comes up

"$1,200 for a one-pager, $2,250 for multi-page. Hosting and maintenance is $129 or $149 a month. But honestly — look at it first. If it doesn't feel worth it, the conversation stops there."

Objections — tap to expand
Don't fight it — agree, then reframe.

"Good — that's actually the best time to do this. You're not making decisions out of desperation, you can be selective. What a website really does for a busy guy is raise the floor on the kind of work that comes in. People who find you online and do their homework before calling tend to be easier customers — less price haggling, clearer on what they want. It's not about more work, it's about better work."

Respect it — word of mouth is genuinely valuable.

"It does — and I'm not trying to replace it. The thing about word of mouth is it sends whoever it sends. A website gives you a little more control over who finds you and what they think before they call. People who find you through a website tend to come in more pre-sold, less price sensitive. The two work well together — word of mouth builds trust, a website lets strangers find that trust on their own."

Don't knock it.

"Facebook's great for staying in front of people who already know you. The difference is a cold stranger who searches for [trade] and lands on your Facebook page — they're not in buying mode on Facebook, they're in scrolling mode. A website is purpose-built for someone who's actively looking to hire. It answers the right questions in the right order and makes it easy to take the next step."

Most common thing I hear.

"Totally makes sense — it always feels like it can wait. What I'm offering takes that off the table. I do the work first. You don't manage anything or deal with back-and-forth — you just open a link and tell me what you think. I'm trying to make the starting part free."

Good sign — answer it straight.

"One-page site is $1,200, multi-page is $2,250. Hosting and maintenance after that is $129 a month. But I lead with 'see it first' because I'd rather you make that call based on what you're actually getting, not just a number."

Respect it immediately.

"Totally fair. One thing I'll say before I let you go — there's nothing to commit to upfront. But I understand if it's not the right time. Would it be alright if I checked back in a month or so?"

If they say no, just say "understood, good luck with the season" and move on.
Don't get defensive. Agree with them.

"I get that — I'd probably think the same thing. The hardest part of my job is getting someone to trust a stranger enough to take a chance. So I started building it first, at my cost, because it removes that whole barrier. There's nothing in this for me unless you're genuinely happy with it."

Closing — permission, not pressure
Best close

"Give me the go-ahead and I'll have something for you to look at by [day]. You don't have to commit to anything — just willing to take 5 minutes to look at a link. Is that something you'd be open to?"

If interested but hesitant

"What would need to be true for this to feel worth a look? I'd rather just answer that directly."

If they want time to think

"That makes sense. Can I call you back [day]? I'll keep it short."

If they say yes

"Perfect. I just need your business name, a quick description of what you do, and any photos you've got. If you don't have photos, I can work with what's public. I'll send you a link by [day]."

Never say

"So are we doing this?" / "Do we have a deal?" — kills the whole tone.

Tone and presence
Be this

Calm. Curious. Talking to someone who's doing fine — not rescuing them.

Not this

Urgent. Pitchy. Implying they're missing out or falling behind.

Their reality

They're probably busy. Word of mouth works. Don't pretend otherwise.

Your angle

Not more business — better business. Customers who arrive pre-sold.

Silence

After a question — slow down. Let them fill it. The pause is doing work.

Firm no

"I appreciate you picking up" — and move on. No re-pitch.

The core argument

Word of mouth sends whoever it sends. A website lets better customers find you on their own terms — they research, they decide, and they call ready to hire. Less price shopping, less back-and-forth, better jobs. It runs in the background without them doing anything differently.
