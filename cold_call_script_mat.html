Cold call script system

Local service businesses — no website

Openings — pick one, say it like a thought
A — Default

"Hey, is this [business]? My name's Mat, I'm a web designer locally. I came across your business doing some research — you've got strong reviews but no website. I had a thought I wanted to run by you — got about a minute?"

B — Lean into curiosity

"Hey, this might be a little random — I'm Mat, I do web design. I was looking at [trade] companies in the area and yours stood out. Reviews are genuinely good, but there's no website. Is that intentional, or just something that hasn't happened yet?"

C — If they sound busy or guarded

"I'll keep it short — I'm Mat, I build websites for local businesses. Yours caught my eye because your Google reputation is solid but there's no site behind it. I had an idea — is now a decent time?"

D — If they ask "what's this about"

"Yeah, fair — I'm Mat, I build websites. Not trying to sell you anything right now. I noticed your reviews are strong and there's no website, and I had an idea I wanted to share. No pitch, I promise — just a thought."

Lead with what you noticed, not what you sell. You're calling because something specific caught your attention.

The core observation
Full version

"You've got reviews — specific ones, where people talk about the actual experience. That's real word-of-mouth. But when someone finds you online and lands on a Facebook page or a Google listing, they're in someone else's space — ads, their layout, whatever else is going on. A website is the one place online that's completely yours. You control what they see, how you explain your services, how they reach you."

Short version

"You're findable — but findable and compelling aren't the same thing. A website is where you actually get to make the case."

By trade
HVAC

"When someone's furnace goes out at 9pm and they Google HVAC, they might find your listing — but then what? They see a Google profile with your number and some reviews. A website is where you make the case for why they should call you instead of the next guy. The listing gets you found. The website gets you chosen."

Lawn care

"Lawn care bookings happen in late winter — people go online, compare, check reviews. You've got the reviews, there's just nothing to land on that tells your story. A Google listing is a starting point. A website is where someone actually decides to call you."

Painting

"People can see some photos on your Google listing or Yelp — but those platforms control everything around them. Ads, competitor listings, whatever reviews they want to surface. A website is where you show your best work the way you'd actually want someone to see it. You're telling your own story instead of letting someone else's platform tell it."

Pest control

"Your Facebook page probably does show up in search — that's good. But when someone clicks and lands there, they're in Facebook's world. A website is the one place that's just about you: your services, your process, how to reach you. Found is not the same as yours."

The transition

"Here's why I'm calling — and this is a little different from how this usually goes. I'm not going to ask you to commit to anything. I'd rather just build it first, and then let you decide."

The offer
Standard

"I'll build you a fully custom site — designed for your business, your services, your reviews — in three business days. Nothing out of your pocket. Once it's done, I'll send you a link. You look at it, if it feels right we talk. If not, no hard feelings. You've got nothing to lose by just seeing it."

Short version

"I build it, you look at it, you decide. Three days, nothing owed."

They're not saying yes to buying a website. They're saying yes to looking at something that already exists. Completely different ask.

If price comes up

"$1,200 for a one-pager, $2,250 for multi-page. Hosting and maintenance is $129 or $149 a month. But honestly — look at it first. If it doesn't feel worth it, the conversation stops there."

Objections — tap to expand
Validate it — genuinely.

"That's great to hear — and I'm not trying to fix something that isn't broken. The only thing I'd say is that the businesses I've seen get hit hardest are the ones that were busy right up until a slow stretch hit. A website isn't just about getting more calls — it's also there when things get quieter. But if now's not the time, I get it."

Then pause. Let them fill the silence.
Don't knock it — and don't claim it doesn't show up in search.

"That makes sense — and your Facebook page probably does show up when people search for you. The gap isn't really about being found, it's about what happens after they click. They land on Facebook and they're in Facebook's world — ads, their layout, whatever else is on the feed. A website is the one place that's completely yours. You decide what they see, how you explain your services, how they reach you."

Most common thing I hear.

"Totally makes sense — it always feels like it can wait. What I'm offering takes that off the table. I do the work first. You don't manage anything or deal with back-and-forth — you just open a link and tell me what you think. I'm trying to make the starting part free."

Good sign — answer it straight.

"One-page site is $1,200, multi-page is $2,250. Hosting and maintenance after that is $129 a month. But I lead with 'see it first' because I'd rather you make that call based on what you're actually getting, not just a number."

Respect it immediately.

"Totally fair. One thing I'll say before I let you go — there's nothing to commit to upfront. But I understand if it's not the right time. Would it be alright if I checked back in a month or so?"

If they say no, just say "understood, good luck with the season" and move on.
Don't get defensive. Agree with them.

"I get that — I'd probably think the same thing. The hardest part of my job is getting someone to trust a stranger enough to take a chance. So I started building it first, at my cost, because it removes that whole barrier. There's nothing in this for me unless you're genuinely happy with it."

Closing — permission, not pressure
Best close

"Give me the go-ahead and I'll have something for you to look at by [day]. You don't have to commit to anything — just willing to take 5 minutes to look at a link. Is that something you'd be open to?"

If interested but hesitant

"What would need to be true for this to feel worth a look? I'd rather just answer that directly."

If they want time to think

"That makes sense. Can I call you back [day]? I'll keep it short."

If they say yes

"Perfect. I just need your business name, a quick description of what you do, and any photos you've got. If you don't have photos, I can work with what's public. I'll send you a link by [day]."

Never say

"So are we doing this?" / "Do we have a deal?" — kills the whole tone.

Tone and presence
Be this

Calm. Curious. Like you noticed something and wanted to share it.

Not this

Urgent. Pitchy. "Limited-time opportunity." Never.

Your role

A perceptive local professional who noticed a gap. Not a salesperson with a quota.

Their experience

They should feel seen, not sold. Like you actually looked at their business.

Silence

After a question — slow down. Let them fill it. The pause is doing work.

Firm no

"I appreciate you picking up" — and move on. No re-pitch.

The core argument

Being found and being chosen are two different things. A Google listing or Facebook page gets you found. A website is where you get chosen. Don't overclaim visibility they already have — that's the argument that gets caught.
